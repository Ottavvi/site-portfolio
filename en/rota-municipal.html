Case study

Rota Municipal.

A system for Brazilian city halls to manage their whole fleet in one place: vehicles, drivers, school buses, patient transport, fuel and maintenance, with a driver app that works even without signal in rural areas.

The problem

Public fleets run on spreadsheets and notebooks.

In many small Brazilian towns, each department manages its own vehicles its own way. That shows up as concrete problems: vehicles driving with expired documents, uncontrolled fuel spending, school routes with no driver on the day, trouble reporting to federal school-transport programs and audit courts, and no idea where each vehicle is.

The challenge wasn't only technical. Users range from the transportation manager at a desk to the school bus driver on a phone, on a dirt road with no signal. And some of the data is sensitive: who travels for medical treatment and which children ride the school bus.

Who it is for

Five roles, each seeing only what it needs.

Role What they do What they can't see
Transportation manager Records, request approvals, work orders, traffic fines, audit log Names of patients and students (counts only)
Health department Patient transport for out-of-town treatment, waiting list, WhatsApp confirmation Other departments' vehicles
Education department School routes, students, bus roll call, attendance Patient data
Other departments Vehicle requests, trips and refueling for their own department Other departments' data
Driver Phone app: pre-trip checklist, trips, refueling with photo, incident reports Passengers' medical specialty and phone number

These rules are enforced on the server, not just in the UI: a direct API call with the wrong role is rejected.

Technical decisions

Choices designed for a small city hall.

The person installing and maintaining the system is often the city hall's own IT technician. Every decision below aims to keep that job simple and safe.

SQLite built into Node

No database server to install or maintain: the database is a file. The daily backup copies it along with the photos, with an encrypted copy off the server, and every Monday the system rehearses a restore and flags the dashboard if anything fails.

Numbered migrations

The database is created and evolves through 33 SQL files applied in order at startup. A city hall already using the system only receives what is missing, without losing data.

Business rules on the server

A vehicle with expired registration or a failed checklist can't leave. A driver with an expired license or drug test is blocked. School routes require a bus license and specific training. No vehicle or driver can be booked twice at the same time.

React screens, loaded on demand

There are 30 screens, but each one is only downloaded when opened. The driver's phone never loads the back-office screens, and the map, the heaviest part, only reaches people who use it.

The biggest challenge

An app that works without signal.

In rural areas, drivers start the school route where there's no internet. If the app depended on a connection, the checklist and trip start would never be recorded.

  1. Saves on the device, right away. Every action (checklist, trip start, refueling) goes into a queue on the phone, with the real date and time it happened.
  2. Photos in IndexedDB. The browser's regular storage is too small for receipt and dashboard photos.
  3. Syncs when the connection returns. It goes in batches, without the driver doing anything.
  4. Never duplicates. Each action has a unique ID; if a sync drops halfway and is retried, the server recognizes it and does not record it twice.

This flow is tested automatically: an end-to-end test simulates a phone with no signal, does the checklist and starts the trip, restores the connection and checks that everything reached the server.

Data protection

Privacy from day one.

The system stores health data (the medical specialty for each treatment trip) and children's data. So protection under LGPD, Brazil's data protection law, was part of the design, not an afterthought:

  • Patient data is visible only to the Health department; the manager only sees how many travel.
  • The system doesn't store national ID or health card numbers, diagnoses or drug test results, only what is necessary.
  • An audit log of who did what and when, including every lookup of patient data, without recording names or specialties.
  • Data export for a patient or student, and anonymization for those who left the service.
  • Passwords hashed with scrypt, lockout after 5 failed attempts, HttpOnly cookies, a Content Security Policy and self-hosted fonts, so users' IPs aren't shared with third parties.
  • The driver's location is only recorded during trips and is deleted after 90 days.
Quality

Tested on every change.

135 API tests (rules, permissions, security)
30 end-to-end tests, on desktop and mobile
27 API modules
5 years of simulated data in the performance test

On every push to GitHub, CI runs ESLint, checks formatting, runs the API tests, builds the frontend and opens the system in a real browser to use it like a person would. One test checks that the heaviest queries use database indexes: without them, with 5 years of history and 80 vehicles, screens like cost per vehicle took over half a second.

What I learned

Public software is about trust.

The most important lesson wasn't a technology, but thinking about the people who use it: a rule that only exists in the UI can be bypassed; a backup that was never restored may not work; an app that needs internet fails exactly where it's needed most.

The next step is a pilot: the system has a six-week rollout plan, starting with one department, with role-based training and spreadsheet import of existing records.
